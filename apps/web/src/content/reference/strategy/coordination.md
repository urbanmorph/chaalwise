In The Strategy of Conflict (1960), Thomas Schelling set people problems they could win only by matching a partner they could not talk to. Told to name heads or tails, 36 of his 42 respondents said heads. He also asked students where and when to meet someone in New York, with no plan agreed. Most chose the information booth at Grand Central Station, at noon. Those answers were no better in themselves. They simply stood out, and each person expected the other to expect them. Schelling called such an answer a focal point.

Judith Mehta, Chris Starmer and Robert Sugden (1994) tested this. Some people were told only to pick an answer; others were told to try to match a partner. Those trying to match chose the obvious answer more often, and matched more often. In a later summary, 87% of those trying to match said heads. The lesson is to ask not "which do I like?" but "which will we all see?".

In his Discourse on Inequality (1755), Jean-Jacques Rousseau told of a group of hunters. They could catch a stag (a large deer) only if every one of them held his post. But any one of them could catch a hare alone. Brian Skyrms, in The Stag Hunt and the Evolution of Social Structure (2004), took the story as a model of social cooperation. Going for the stag is best if everyone does. Going for the hare is safe whatever the others do. Both are stable, and which one a group lands on depends on trust.

In the lab, trust often loses. John Van Huyck, Raymond Battalio and Richard Beil (1990) had groups of people each choose an effort level from 1 to 7. Pay went up with the lowest effort anyone in the group chose, and down with your own effort. So any effort above the group's lowest was wasted. Large groups slid quickly to the lowest, worst outcome; smaller groups did better. One cautious person sets the result for all. Talk can help. Russell Cooper and colleagues (1992) studied games where one choice was safer but the other paid more. When both players could send each other a message before playing, they always reached the better outcome.

Words are cheap, though. Someone who was let down before may need more than a promise: a commitment they can see, or a track record of the plan working. These are lab and classroom findings, a guide to how groups behave rather than a rule for every team.
