In poker, every bet tells the other players something about your cards. A bluff is a bet with a weak hand, made to get a stronger hand to give up (fold). It works only when the story your bets tell is believable. The poker lessons [Why you bet](/poker/why-you-bet/) and [Signals and tests](/poker/signals-and-tests/) show this at the table.

Away from the table, the economist Michael Spence (1973) asked why employers trust a degree. His answer was that a degree costs effort, and it costs less for people suited to the work. So earning one sorts people, even if the course teaches nothing used on the job. The biologist Amotz Zahavi (1975) made the same point about a peacock's tail. Only a healthy bird can afford to carry it, so the tail is believed.

A claim that costs nothing to make, true or false, is called cheap talk. It is not worthless: people often tell the truth when their interests match yours. But when they gain from you believing it, weigh it lightly, and ask what they would lose if it were false.

In poker, bluffing is part of the game, and everyone at the table knows it. Off the table, the useful lesson is the other side of it: spot the cheap claim, and back your own word with something you would lose.
