In 1907 Francis Galton looked at 787 tickets from a contest at a country fair, where each person paid to guess the weight of an ox once it was slaughtered and dressed. The middle guess was 1,207 pounds; the true weight was 1,198. The crowd, taken together, was within 1% of the truth, closer than most of the people in it. This is the wisdom of crowds, and it works best when the guesses are independent: each person's error is their own, so the errors pull different ways and cancel.

Hearing others first spoils it. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman (1974) showed that people's estimates are pulled towards a number they have just seen, even one they know is arbitrary: anchoring. Jan Lorenz and colleagues (2011) let people see each other's estimates between rounds; their guesses drew closer together, which made them more confident, but the group's answer got no more accurate.

Daniel Kahneman, Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein, in Noise (2021), call the scatter of judgements that should agree noise, and recommend collecting independent judgements before any discussion. Irving Janis, in his study of groupthink in policy teams (1982), advised leaders not to state their own view at the outset, so that others do not simply line up behind it.

Averaging does not fix everything. If everyone shares the same blind spot, the average shares it too. But for estimates where people err in different directions, the cheapest improvement a group can make is to guess alone first, then pool.
