In 1907 Francis Galton studied a contest at a country fair. Each person paid to guess the weight of an ox once it was slaughtered and prepared as meat. He looked at 787 of the tickets. The middle guess was 1,207 pounds, and the true weight was 1,198. The crowd, taken together, was within 1% of the truth, closer than most of the people in it. This is called the wisdom of crowds. It works best when the guesses are independent, made without hearing each other. Then each person's error is their own, so the errors pull different ways and cancel.

Hearing one voice first spoils it. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman (1974) showed that people's estimates are pulled towards a number they have just seen, even one they know means nothing. This is called anchoring. Jan Lorenz and colleagues (2011) let people see each other's estimates between rounds. Their guesses drew closer together, and that made them more confident. But the group's answer got no more accurate. Later work found that sharing estimates can help when every voice counts equally. But one dominant voice drags the group towards its own error (Becker and colleagues 2017).

In their book Noise (2021), Daniel Kahneman, Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein use "noise" for the scatter in judgements that should agree. They recommend collecting independent judgements before any discussion. Irving Janis studied groupthink in policy teams (1982): a group agreeing too quickly to avoid conflict. He advised leaders not to state their own view at the start, so that others do not simply line up behind it.

Averaging does not fix everything. If everyone shares the same blind spot, the average shares it too. But when people's errors go in different directions, one of the cheapest things a group can do is guess alone first, then pool the guesses.
