Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman (1981) asked people to choose between two plans against a disease expected to kill 600 people. Put as lives saved, plan A saves 200 for sure. Plan B has a 1 in 3 chance of saving all 600, and a 2 in 3 chance of saving none. 72% chose the sure plan. Put as deaths, plan A means 400 die for sure. Plan B has a 1 in 3 chance that nobody dies, and a 2 in 3 chance that all 600 die. Now 78% chose the gamble. The plans were the same; only the description changed.

Their earlier work, prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky 1979), explains why. People judge an outcome as a gain or a loss from a starting point (a reference point), not by where they end up. Each extra gain pleases less than the one before. So saving 600 does not feel three times as good as saving 200, and the sure 200 wins. Each extra loss hurts less than the one before. So losing 600 does not feel 1.5 times as bad as losing 400, and the gamble wins. Framing (the way a choice is worded) moves the reference point, and the choice moves with it. Separately, a loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases: about twice as much, in later estimates (Tversky and Kahneman 1992).

It is not only students in a laboratory. Barbara McNeil and colleagues (1982) asked patients, students and doctors to imagine choosing a treatment for lung cancer. All three groups chose surgery more often when its results were given as the chance of surviving, not the chance of dying. The figures were the same.

Neither plan is wrong in itself: in the example above, both keep the same amount on average. What is hard to defend is choosing differently because of the words. The simple check is to say the choice both ways, or as plain end results, and see if your answer stays the same. For example: "we end with 200 people saved for sure, or a 1 in 3 chance of all 600". It will not remove the pull of a loss, but it shows you when the wording is doing the deciding.
