In 1973 Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that people judge how likely something is by how well a case fits a picture. They largely ignore how common each possibility is. Given a description that sounded like an engineer, people guessed "engineer" whether they were told the group was 30% or 70% engineers.

The fix is to take the outside view first. Kahneman and Dan Lovallo (1993) describe it. Find a group of similar past cases (a reference class), see how they turned out, and start there. Only then adjust for what you know about this case. Roger Buehler and colleagues (1994) asked students how long their theses would take. Their best guess was 34 days on average, but they took 55. Fewer than half finished even by their worst-case date. The outside view would have warned them.

Counting helps. Rather than percentages, imagine 100 or 1,000 cases and count how many end each way. The answer is often far from what the evidence alone suggests.
