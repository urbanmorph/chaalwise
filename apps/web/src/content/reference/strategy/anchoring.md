Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman (1974) spun a wheel of numbers in front of people, then asked what share of the countries in the United Nations were African. The wheel was set to stop at 10 or at 65. People who saw 10 gave a typical answer of 25%; people who saw 65 gave 45%. The wheel could not have told them anything, yet it moved their answers. This is called anchoring: a first number pulls the estimates that follow it, like an anchor holding a boat.

Knowing the subject does not stop it. Gregory Northcraft and Margaret Neale (1987) showed estate agents round a house for sale, each with a different asking price. The agents' own valuations rose and fell with the asking price, though few of them named it as something they had weighed.

Why does it happen? To check a first number, people look for reasons it could be right, and those reasons then shape their own estimate. Thomas Mussweiler, Fritz Strack and Tim Pfeiffer (2000) asked car experts to price a used car after hearing a seller's figure. Experts who first listed reasons why that figure might be wrong were pulled less. This fix is called "consider the opposite". The simple version: before you estimate, give one reason the number could be too high and one it could be too low.

Negotiation runs on the same pull. Adam Galinsky and Thomas Mussweiler (2001) found that the first offer strongly predicted the final price, so the side that made it usually did better. The advantage disappeared when the other side thought about the first side's walk-away point and other options, or about their own target. So make the first offer when you know what the thing is worth. When the other side names a number first, do not bargain from it: put your own number on the table, with your reasons.
