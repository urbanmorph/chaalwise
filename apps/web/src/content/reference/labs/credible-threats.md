Words cost nothing, so a threat counts only if the person making it would carry it out when the time comes. Take a tea stall owner who says, "Open beside me and I will halve my prices until you close." Look past the words to the moment after you open. If a price war would cost the owner more than sharing the customers, the owner will share, and the threat was empty. Reinhard Selten (1965) made this a test for any game where players take turns. Each player's plan must still be their best choice at every point the game can reach. A plan that relies on a threat nobody would carry out fails the test.

A threat can be made believable by changing what carrying it out is worth. In The Strategy of Conflict (1960), Thomas Schelling showed that a player can gain by tying their own hands. They give up a choice, in a way the other side can see, so that carrying out the threat becomes their best move. Burning your bridges, so that you cannot retreat, is the old picture of it. Avinash Dixit (1980) showed how this works between firms. A firm that builds a large plant before a rival arrives will want to fill it. So its promise to fight for every customer becomes believable, and the rival may stay away.

In Thinking Strategically (1991), Avinash Dixit and Barry Nalebuff list the usual ways to make a move binding. Sign a contract that is costly to break, stake a good name you would lose, or take a step that cannot be undone. A commitment that can be cancelled for free changes nothing. And it has to come first: a commitment works only if it is made, and seen, before the other side decides. This is one way that moving first can help.

Commitment has a cost. It removes your choices, and if the deal you sign costs more than the threat saves, you are worse off than if you had shared. Ask what the commitment costs, as well as what it prevents.
