Two animals want the same patch of ground. Neither fights; each simply stays, and staying costs it time and food it could have found elsewhere. Whoever stays longer gets the patch. John Maynard Smith (1974) called this a war of attrition. The loser gets nothing and has still paid for all the time it stayed, and so has the winner.

Between people it is a stand-off where each day of waiting costs both sides. Think of two candidates who each wait for the other to withdraw, a strike, a family dispute, or two shops each waiting for the other to close. Each side wants the other to give up first, and the longer it goes on, the more both have paid.

What if both are equally ready to hold out? Maynard Smith found, and D. T. Bishop and C. Cannings (1978) proved more generally, that the stable way to play is at random. Give up in any week with the same small chance, however long you have held out. Then every plan does as well as any other, and on average the cost of holding out eats the whole prize. With a prize of 10 points and a cost of 1 point a week, the chance is 1 in 11 a week, and each side ends with 0 on average. In about 1 contest in 3, the two sides pay more between them than the prize is worth.

Three lessons follow. First, set your stop point before you start, from what the prize is worth to you and what you know of the other side. Once the contest is on, what you have spent is gone whatever you do. The only question each week is whether the next one is worth it (see sunk cost). Second, signals of staying power matter, but only those that cost the sender. Anyone can say they will never give up; paying up front for a long stay is believable, because it makes sense only to someone who means to stay. Third, the best way out of a war of attrition is often not to fight it. A vote, taking turns or splitting the prize costs both sides less than waiting each other out.
