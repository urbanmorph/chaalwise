Most deals have more than one issue. A job offer has a salary, a joining date, days at home and a training budget. A supplier deal has a price, payment terms and delivery dates. The two sides rarely care about each issue equally.

Howard Raiffa, in The Art and Science of Negotiation (1982), gave each side a scoring sheet: points for each option on each issue. He showed that bargaining issue by issue, meeting halfway on each, can end with fewer points in all than the two sides could have had. Trading does better. Each side gives way on the issues it cares less about, in return for the ones it cares more about. Both sides gain, because the deal itself grows. This is called logrolling, or integrative negotiation.

To trade, you need to know what the other side cares about. Roger Fisher and William Ury, in Getting to Yes (1981), separate positions from interests. A position is what someone asks for, such as "join in one week". An interest is why they ask, such as "our project starts this month". Two positions can clash while the interests behind them fit together. So ask why, and say why you want what you want.

Before you trade, know your walk-away point: what you get with no deal. A trade is worth making only if the whole package beats it.
