Take a committee of three equal groups choosing among three options. The first group ranks A, then B, then C. The second ranks B, then C, then A. The third ranks C, then A, then B. Put any two options to a vote and a majority picks one: A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A. The majority's choices go round in a circle, so no option beats both of the others. The Marquis de Condorcet described this in 1785, and it is called the Condorcet paradox. Each person's ranking makes sense on its own; only the group's does not.

Kenneth Arrow, in Social Choice and Individual Values (1951), showed that this is not a quirk of one voting method. No way of turning people's rankings into a group ranking meets a few fair-sounding conditions in every case. Some groups, some of the time, will have no clear majority choice.

When majorities cycle, the order of the votes decides. Committees often vote on two options first, then put the winner against the third. If everyone votes for the one they like more each time, the option held back to the last vote wins. So whoever sets the order can choose any of the three. Richard McKelvey (1976) showed that when majorities cycle across many options, a chair who controls the order can lead a group to almost any result. William Riker, in The Art of Political Manipulation (1986), collected real cases of people winning by setting the agenda, choosing what is on the shortlist, or adding a new option. A shortlist works the same way: leave one option off, and the vote between the other two is decided before it starts.

Members can answer by looking ahead. Robin Farquharson, in Theory of Voting (1969), studied voters who reason back from the last vote. A sincere vote is for the option you like more. A strategic vote is for another option, to stop a worse one winning later. It pays only when you can see that a sincere vote in the first round leads to a worse result in the last. In the last vote, a sincere vote is always best.
