In chapter 12 of The General Theory (1936), the economist John Maynard Keynes compared investing to a newspaper contest. Readers pick the six prettiest faces from a hundred photographs, and the prize goes to those whose picks are closest to the most popular. So you should not pick the faces you like best, nor even those you think others like best. You should pick those you think others expect others to like. Keynes called this the third degree, and said some people go on to the fourth, fifth and higher.

Rosemarie Nagel (1995) tested the idea in the laboratory with a simple game. Everyone picks a number from 0 to 100, and the winner is closest to two-thirds of the average. Reason it through. Even if everyone picked 100, two-thirds of the average would be 67, so no one should pick more than 67. If no one does, no one should pick more than 44, then 30, and so on, down to 0. Game theory calls 0 the equilibrium: the one answer that is still best when everyone knows everyone else reasons the same way.

But people do not reason all the way. In the first round of Nagel's two-thirds games, the guesses averaged about 37. Many sat near 33 and 22: one or two steps of two-thirds from 50. So the winning number was about 24, and a guess of 0 would have lost by a long way. When the same people played again, the average fell round after round, as each player expected the others to think one step further.

Level-k models describe this. A level 0 player picks without thinking, at random. A level 1 player expects everyone else to be level 0 and picks two-thirds of 50, about 33. A level 2 player expects level 1 players and picks about 22, and so on. Colin Camerer, Teck-Hua Ho and Juin-Kuan Chong (2004) looked across many games. People thought about one and a half steps ahead on average, and few went beyond three. The lesson is not to stop thinking early. It is to think about how far the others think, and to go one step beyond them, which may be short of the "rational" answer.
