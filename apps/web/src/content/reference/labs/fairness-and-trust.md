In the ultimatum game, one player offers how to split a sum, and the other can only accept or refuse. A refusal leaves both with nothing. If people counted only money, the second player would take any offer above nothing, so the first would offer the smallest amount possible. Werner Güth, Rolf Schmittberger and Bernd Schwarze (1982) tried it with students. The most common offer was half, and some players turned down low offers, giving up money to do so. Many later studies, summed up by Colin Camerer (2003), found the same. Most offers are 40 to 50 per cent, and offers under 20 per cent are turned down about half the time. Fairness is a real force: an offer that leaves the other side feeling cheated can cost you the deal.

In the trust game, one player can send some of their money to a partner. Whatever is sent is tripled, and the partner then decides how much to send back. Joyce Berg, John Dickhaut and Kevin McCabe (1995) gave each sender 10 dollars. If everyone looked after only themselves, partners would send nothing back, so nobody would send anything. In fact almost all senders sent something, about half of their 10 dollars on average. What came back varied widely: some partners returned more than they were sent, others little or nothing.

Trust makes more for both sides to share, but it pays only with people who pay it back. Answering trust with trust is called reciprocity, and it is what makes trust work. A record of how someone treated you is the best guide to how far to trust them next time. With a stranger, trust in small steps. And be someone worth trusting yourself: people send more to those who returned it before.
