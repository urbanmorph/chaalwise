In American slang, a lemon is a car that turns out to be bad. The economist George Akerlof (1970) asked what happens in a used-car market where sellers know whether their car is a lemon and buyers cannot tell. Buyers will pay only what an average car is worth. That is too little for the owner of a good car, who keeps it. With fewer good cars on sale, the average falls, and so does what buyers will pay. In the worst case only lemons are left. This is called adverse selection: the price itself selects the sellers you least want.

Two ways out let quality show. In screening, the side that knows less pays to find out, or sets a test that sorts people. A mechanic's check, a trial task at an interview or a medical check before insurance all screen. Michael Rothschild and Joseph Stiglitz (1976) showed how an insurer can offer a choice of policies that leads each customer to show their own risk.

In signalling, the side that knows more does something that would cost them dearly if they were lying. Michael Spence (1973) used a degree as his example. A warranty works the same way. It costs the seller of a good scooter little, since it rarely breaks down. It costs the seller of a poor one a lot. So only good sellers offer it, and buyers can believe it. "It runs like new" costs nothing to say, true or false, so it tells you little. In poker, a bet tells a story the same way: see the lesson [A bet tells a story](/skills/a-bet-tells-a-story/).
